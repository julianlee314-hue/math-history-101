Concept 001

Counting

I · Origins
一1 · MARK三3 · ALGORITHM六6 · COMPUTE
一

1 · MARK

MesopotamiaEgypt/Eastern Mediterranean

Long before writing, people needed a way to keep track of “how many” when memory alone would not do—animals, days, bundles of grain. The oldest candidates are notched bones: the Lebombo bone from southern Africa (roughly 43,000–44,000 years ago) and the better-known Ishango bone from the Congo region (about 20,000–25,000 years ago). Scholars still debate how much arithmetic those notches encode, but the basic move is clear: one mark for one thing. That one-to-one pairing is counting’s first technology. Mathera’s tally stick invites the same ancient gesture—make a mark, match it to the world—before symbols ever enter the picture.

三

3 · ALGORITHM

ChinaIndiaMesopotamia

Once tallies piled up, cultures invented faster procedures. Mesopotamian scribes grouped marks by sixties on clay accounting boards; Chinese clerks slid counting rods into decimal columns; Indian teachers and later Islamic arithmeticians drilled place-by-place methods any apprentice could repeat. The abacus turned “how many?” into a choreography of hands: fill a place, carry to the next, clear, continue. Counting stopped being only a heap of notches and became a teachable recipe—something a merchant house or tax office could standardize across workers. That procedural turn is why later machines found counting so easy to automate: the steps were already lined up waiting for gears and circuits.

六

6 · COMPUTE

American/Global ComputationalBritish Industrial/Engineering

Electronic machines made counting automatic without changing what a count means. Nineteenth-century punched cards tallied censuses; mechanical registers clicked along shop counters; mid-twentieth-century computers began incrementing registers billions of times a second. Spreadsheets, databases, and digital sensors still rest on the ancient idea—one unit, one record—only now the tally stick is silicon and the notch is a bit. What changed is not the meaning of “how many,” but who or what keeps the tally without fatigue. Digital counting inherited the MARK-era promise and the ALGORITHM-era recipe, then removed the human hand from the loop while leaving the one-to-one pairing intact.
