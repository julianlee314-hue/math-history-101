Concept 001

Counting

I · Origins
一1 · MARK三3 · ALGORITHM六6 · COMPUTE
一

1 · MARK

MesopotamiaEgypt/Eastern Mediterranean

People needed a way to track “how many” when memory alone failed—animals, days, grain. Early candidates are notched bones: the Lebombo bone (about 43,000–44,000 years ago) and the Ishango bone (about 20,000–25,000 years ago). Scholars debate how much arithmetic those notches encode, but the basic move is clear: one mark for one thing. That one-to-one pairing is counting’s first technology. Mathera’s tally stick invites the same gesture before symbols appear.

三

3 · ALGORITHM

ChinaIndiaMesopotamia

Once tallies piled up, cultures invented faster procedures. Mesopotamian scribes grouped marks by sixties; Chinese clerks used counting rods in decimal columns; Indian and later Islamic teachers drilled place-by-place methods. The abacus turned counting into a hand recipe: fill a place, carry, clear, continue. Counting became a teachable algorithm merchant houses could standardize—steps later machines would automate.

六

6 · COMPUTE

American/Global ComputationalBritish Industrial/Engineering

Machines made counting automatic without changing what a count means. Punched cards tallied censuses; mechanical registers clicked in shops; computers increment registers billions of times a second. Spreadsheets and sensors still rest on one unit, one record—now the tally is silicon and the notch is a bit. Digital counting kept the one-to-one pairing and removed the tired human hand from the loop.
