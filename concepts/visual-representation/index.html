Concept 037

Visual Representation

I · Origins
一1 · MARK二2 · WRITE四4 · PRINT
一

1 · MARK

Egypt/Eastern MediterraneanMesopotamia

Diagrams, sketches, and figure marks made mathematics visible before it was fully verbal. Floor plans, field outlines, and schematic drawings carried geometric information across workers. Visual representation is not decoration; it is a parallel channel for exact-enough meaning when words lag. A wrong diagram could bankrupt a build—liability, not only beauty, in temple and canal projects.

二

2 · WRITE

Greek/Hellenistic

Greek geometry’s diagrams were partners to proof—letters on figures tied sentences to lines. Manuscripts taught readers to see necessity in a drawing. From Euclid to later illustrated commentaries, eyes and reason shared the work. Manuscripts that omitted figures often became cryptic; the visual channel was load-bearing for geometric meaning, not optional illustration.

四

4 · PRINT

Atlantic Scientific RevolutionBritish Industrial/Engineering

Printed figures, plates, and classroom manipulatives multiplied visual access. Engineering drawing standards made visual representation a contract. Copperplate engravings and later textbook lithography raised the baseline quality of what students saw. Flip between picture and symbol, and understanding often clicks where prose alone stalled. Students could be held responsible for what printed figures made visible.
