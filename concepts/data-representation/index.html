Concept 015

Data Representation

I · Origins
一1 · MARK三3 · ALGORITHM六6 · COMPUTE
一

1 · MARK

Mesopotamia

Clay tokens and sealed bullae (from the late Neolithic into the fourth millennium BCE in Mesopotamia) stored facts about goods before full writing existed. Marks on ledgers, notches on sticks, and inked columns are the same move: choose a form that stands for a fact so others can read it later. Data representation begins whenever a community refuses to let important quantities vanish with a single person’s memory. The token is not yet a novel; it is already a database in clay.

三

3 · ALGORITHM

Baghdad/Islamic-PersianChinaIndia

Tables—astronomical, commercial, fiscal—organized data into rows you could look up by a taught procedure. Chinese, Indian, and Islamic scientific cultures built dense manuscript tables; clerks learned to find the correct row and apply the matching rule. Representation became actionable: not only stored, but queried on demand. The manuscript table is the ancestor of every lookup modern institutions still run, from tax schedules to star ephemerides. A table without a lookup rule is only a mural; with a rule it becomes a working instrument for prediction and tax alike.

六

6 · COMPUTE

American/Global Computational

Spreadsheets, relational databases, and binary encodings turned data representation into the bloodstream of modern work. Punch cards once marched through national censuses; now files and clouds carry the clay token’s old promise—facts that outlive the moment of seeing. Formats and schemas are today’s sealed bullae: agreements about what the marks mean when someone else, or some other program, opens them. Computation did not invent data; it accelerated who can read and rewrite it. Query languages are the latest lookup rules: ask clearly, retrieve the mark that answers.
