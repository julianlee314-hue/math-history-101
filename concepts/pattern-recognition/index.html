Concept 008

Pattern Recognition

I · Origins
一1 · MARK七7 · INTELLIGENCE
一

1 · MARK

MesopotamiaEgypt/Eastern Mediterranean

Humans spotted patterns in notches, seasons, and craft long before school arithmetic. Some readings of the Ishango bone (c. 20,000–25,000 years ago) see grouped marks that look like more than raw tallies—though that reading stays debated. By the Old Babylonian period (early second millennium BCE), clay tablets show deliberate numerical sequences and multiplication tables. Pattern recognition is the capacity that turns “one, two, three…” into “what comes next?” A series chain in Mathera is a small, tangible version of that ancient habit: notice the rule, predict the next link, and feel mathematics as rhythm rather than rote.

七

7 · INTELLIGENCE

American/Global ComputationalCentral Europe 19th-c Abstract

Modern mathematics and machine learning both amplify the ancient habit: find the rule that fits the marks. Nineteenth-century analysts hunted regularities in infinite series; twentieth-century statisticians fitted models to noisy data; symbolic search engines and neural nets now automate “what comes next?” at scales no scribe’s tablet could hold. Proof assistants and pattern-finding algorithms still begin where the Ishango debate began—asking whether the marks hide a law worth trusting. The human spark remains noticing regularity in the first place; silicon multiplies how far that spark can travel.
