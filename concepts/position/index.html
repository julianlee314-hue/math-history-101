Concept 011

Position

II · Classical
二2 · WRITE四4 · PRINT
二

2 · WRITE

Egypt/Eastern MediterraneanChinaGreek/Hellenistic

Position answers “where?” relative to a frame. Egyptian artists used square grids to keep body proportions consistent in tomb painting; Chinese cartographers mapped land with rectangular grids; Greek astronomers and then Ptolemy adapted coordinate ideas from the sky toward descriptions of the earth. A classroom grid is a miniature of those civic frames: rows and columns turn vague location into an address. Once position is speakable—“two right, three up”—children can plan paths, read maps, and eventually meet graphs and coordinates without feeling that the plane appeared from nowhere.

四

4 · PRINT

Atlantic Scientific RevolutionItalian Commercial/Renaissance

Printed maps, sea charts, and coordinate grids let position travel farther than any single surveyor. Latitude and longitude on paper, then Cartesian planes in analytic geometry, gave earth and idea a shared address system anyone could copy. Graph paper, spreading widely in the nineteenth century, made positional thinking a desk habit for engineers and students alike. When frames print cheaply and consistently, “where?” stops belonging only to the person who walked the land and becomes a common language of location. The PRINT epoch’s gift was multiplying the idea until classrooms and workshops shared one vocabulary.
