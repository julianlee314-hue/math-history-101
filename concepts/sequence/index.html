Sequence

I · Origins

一

1 · MARK

Mesopotamia

A sequence is an ordered list—days of a month, rows of a table, steps of a ritual. Babylonian tablets already show number sequences and progressive problems. Ordering items in time or rank turns a heap into a story with a next. Sequences are how memory and prediction share a spine.

Calendars are civilization-scale sequences: miss the next term and festivals, plantings, and tax days drift into chaos. The MARK epoch’s gift was making the idea durable in matter someone else could inspect later.

三

3 · ALGORITHM

IndiaBaghdad/Islamic-PersianChina

Recursive and arithmetic progressions became objects of calculation in Indian, Chinese, and Islamic mathematics—sums of series, rules for the nth term. Manuscript tables listed sequences you could extend by procedure. Once “what comes next?” has a formula, sequences graduate from habit to algorithm.

Formulas for arithmetic and geometric progressions let clerks jump to a far term without writing every predecessor—sequence as random-access memory. The ALGORITHM epoch’s gift was turning the idea into steps apprentices could repeat without reinventing them. 四

4 · PRINT

Atlantic Scientific RevolutionCentral Europe 19th-c Abstract

Printed analysis treated infinite sequences as central citizens of mathematics, with limits and convergence tests. Schoolbooks drilled finite patterns; research chased infinite ones. The series chain’s simple “next link” habit grew into one of analysis’s main languages.

Cauchy’s and later Weierstrass’s rigor asked which infinite sequences deserve to be called convergent—print carried those tests into every analysis course. The PRINT epoch’s gift was multiplying the idea until classrooms and workshops shared one vocabulary.
