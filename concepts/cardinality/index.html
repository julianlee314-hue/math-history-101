Concept 002

Cardinality

I · Origins
一1 · MARK二2 · WRITE
一

1 · MARK

MesopotamiaEgypt/Eastern Mediterranean

Cardinality is the leap from marking to knowing: the last number you say names the whole set. Shepherds used tally sticks this way for centuries—match mark to sheep, trust the final count. Neolithic Near Eastern clay tokens (roughly 8000–3000 BCE) did similar work for goods. A ten-frame stages the same idea: the last filled cell says “ten,” not ten separate ones. Counting becomes an answer about the whole collection.

二

2 · WRITE

Greek/HellenisticBaghdad/Islamic-Persian

Greek mathematicians treated “how many” as something you could reason about—finite collections, magnitudes, and the border with infinity. Euclid kept size talk inside proof. In the nineteenth century, Cantor’s set theory made cardinality precise: two sets share a size when you can pair their members one-to-one, even if both are infinite. The shepherd’s last notch and transfinite sizes share that pairing idea.
