Concept 040

Mathematical Modelling

I · Origins
一1 · MARK五5 · MECHANIZE六6 · COMPUTE
一

1 · MARK

MesopotamiaEgypt/Eastern Mediterranean

Modelling is using mathematics as a stand-in for the world—grain problems, land shapes, ration plans. Babylonian and Egyptian exercise texts already idealize messy reality into solvable forms. A model is a deliberate simplification you agree to trust for a purpose, then revise when the world talks back.

School problem texts that pretend a field is a perfect trapezoid already teach modelling ethics: name your idealization or be ruled by it silently. The MARK epoch’s gift was making the idea durable in matter someone else could inspect later.

五

5 · MECHANIZE

Atlantic Scientific RevolutionBritish Industrial/Engineering

Scientific Revolution models of motion and light, then industrial models of machines and markets, made modelling an explicit craft. Model benches, scale experiments, and equations shared the load. Naming “mathematical modelling” in modern pedagogy only labeled a habit as old as applied problem texts.

Ballistic tables and steam-engine indicators were models with dials—mathematics welded to instruments that talked back with friction and heat. The MECHANIZE epoch’s gift was embodying the idea in instruments that enforced it with gears and scales. 六

6 · COMPUTE

American/Global Computational

Simulation and computational models let parameters sweep through thousands of futures before steel is cut. Modelling now pairs equations with code, but the MARK-era bargain remains: simplify, predict, check against the world, humble the model when it fails.

Climate and epidemiological models now carry public consequences; the old modelling virtues—state assumptions, test, revise—became civic duties. The COMPUTE epoch’s gift was letting machines execute the idea faster than any room of clerks. The COMPUTE epoch’s gift was letting machines execute the idea faster than any room of clerks.
