Concept 048

Logical Statements

II · Classical
二2 · WRITE七7 · INTELLIGENCE
二

2 · WRITE

Greek/Hellenistic

A logical statement is a claim that can be true or false—Aristotle’s organon and Euclidean propositions both depend on assertable sentences. Greek mathematics flourished by writing statements precise enough to prove or refute. Proof seals and scrolls symbolize the moment a claim enters the tribunal of reason.

A proposition’s wording was half the mathematics: vague statements cannot be proved, only gestured at. Greek care with language was a technical invention. The WRITE epoch’s gift was making the idea arguable in sentences and diagrams strangers could check.

七

7 · INTELLIGENCE

American/Global ComputationalCentral Europe 19th-c Abstract

Symbolic logic and proof assistants treat statements as objects machines can check. Frege, Russell, and later type theories made logical form exact enough for silicon scrutiny. The ancient demand—say it so it can be judged—now has automated jurors, while humans still choose which statements matter.

Automated theorem provers still stumble on informal prose; the WRITE-era demand for precise statements remains the bottleneck AI must respect. The INTELLIGENCE epoch’s gift is helping mathematics search and check itself with new kinds of assistants.
