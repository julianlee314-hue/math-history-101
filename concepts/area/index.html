Concept 023

Area

I · Origins
一1 · MARK二2 · WRITE五5 · MECHANIZE
一

1 · MARK

Egypt/Eastern MediterraneanMesopotamia

Area began as field and floor—how much land after the flood, how many bricks for a courtyard. Egyptian and Babylonian problem texts compute areas of rectangles, triangles, and trapezoids with practical rules meant for scribes and surveyors. Exactness varied; some Egyptian circle procedures are known to be approximate, which honest history should say aloud. Still, the civic need was clear: fair shares of land and labor need numbers for surfaces, not only for counts of cattle. When taxes depend on field area, a wrong rule is not a school mistake—it is a political injury.

二

2 · WRITE

Greek/Hellenistic

Euclid and Archimedes made area a matter of proof: congruence arguments, method of exhaustion, and careful comparisons that did not rely on a single formula sheet memorized without reason. Area became something you could argue in a diagram, not only survey with a rope after the flood. Greek writing turned the farmer’s question into a theorem’s conclusion—same practical question, stricter and more portable answer across readers who never saw the original field. Manuscript copies of the Elements carried area arguments into classrooms that had never seen Egyptian flood plains.

五

5 · MECHANIZE

Atlantic Scientific RevolutionBritish Industrial/Engineering

Planimeters traced irregular boundaries and read area off a dial—measurement without tiling the region by hand with unit squares. Analytic geometry and integral calculus treated area as an accumulated product, ready for engineering drawings and machine design. Area-sweep instruments and computed integrals kept the ancient field problem alive on the drafting table, where factories and laboratories needed surface measure as urgently as temples once did. Shipbuilders and surveyors alike needed area tools that worked on curved and irregular outlines, not only neat rectangles.
