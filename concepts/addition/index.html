Concept 006

Addition

I · Origins
一1 · MARK三3 · ALGORITHM六6 · COMPUTE
一

1 · MARK

Egypt/Eastern MediterraneanMesopotamia

Adding is joining quantities. Equal-arm balances in Egypt and Mesopotamia (around the late fourth to early third millennium BCE) matched weights to goods. Egyptian scribes later used doubling and unit-fraction tricks on papyrus. A ten-frame does a classroom version of the same join: fill toward ten, then regroup. Addition unlocked trade, rations, and inventories long before the plus sign.

三

3 · ALGORITHM

ChinaIndiaBaghdad/Islamic-Persian

Column addition on boards and numerals made joining a taught procedure—align places, sum each column, carry the excess. Chinese rod calculus, Indian decimal methods, and later Islamic and European arithmetics polished recipes clerks could run the same way every time. The plus sign is late, but the procedural join is ancient. Once addition was a recipe, apprentices could be drilled and warehouse disputes could shrink.

六

6 · COMPUTE

American/Global ComputationalBritish Industrial/Engineering

Mechanical and electronic adders put the carry chain in gears, relays, and circuits. From Pascal and Leibniz through Babbage to the modern ALU, addition became the machine’s basic verb. Cash registers and spreadsheets still spend huge fractions of their life adding. Every checksum and running balance is still that ancient join—executed without a pan or pebble.
