Concept 006

Addition

I · Origins
一1 · MARK三3 · ALGORITHM六6 · COMPUTE
一

1 · MARK

Egypt/Eastern MediterraneanMesopotamia

Adding is joining quantities—and for millennia the clearest physical model was the balance scale. Equal-arm balances appear in Egypt and Mesopotamia around the turn of the fourth to the third millennium BCE: put weights on one pan until they match the goods on the other. Egyptian scribes later formalized addition with doubling and unit-fraction tricks on papyrus. A ten-frame does a gentler classroom version of the same join: fill toward ten, then regroup. Addition unlocked trade, rations, and building inventories—everyday civilization math—long before anyone wrote an equation with a plus sign.

三

3 · ALGORITHM

ChinaIndiaBaghdad/Islamic-Persian

Column addition on counting boards and written numerals made joining quantities a taught procedure—align places, sum each column, carry the excess to the next. Chinese rod calculus, Indian decimal methods, and later Islamic and European arithmetics polished recipes that tax clerks and merchants could run the same way every time. The plus sign itself is late, arriving with symbolic algebra’s print culture, but the procedural join is ancient. Once addition was a recipe, error could be checked, apprentices could be drilled, and disputes at the warehouse could shrink.

六

6 · COMPUTE

American/Global ComputationalBritish Industrial/Engineering

Mechanical and electronic adders embodied the carry chain in gears, relays, and circuits. From Pascal and Leibniz’s calculating machines through Babbage’s designs to the arithmetic logic unit on a modern chip, addition became the machine’s most basic verb. Cash registers, spreadsheets, and scientific code still spend enormous fractions of their life adding cells and registers. The balance pan’s join survived as voltage and clock cycle: same civic need to combine quantities, new medium that never tires. Every checksum, total, and running balance in modern software is still that ancient join, executed without a pan or a pebble.
