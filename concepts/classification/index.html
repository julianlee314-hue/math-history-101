Concept 009

Classification

I · Origins
一1 · MARK二2 · WRITE
一

1 · MARK

Mesopotamia

Classification sorts the world into kinds—same shape, same size, same use. Neolithic clay tokens (from roughly the eighth millennium BCE onward in the Near East) already classified commodities by form: a cone for one measure, a disk for another. Sorting is how early accounting and early geometry meet: you cannot tax or build fairly unless you can say which things belong together. Shape tiles invite children into that same civic skill—group the triangles, set the circles aside—and quietly prepare the ground for later definitions of square, rectangle, and beyond.

二

2 · WRITE

Greek/HellenisticChina

Definitions and taxonomies made classification speakable across distance. Greek geometry sorted figures by shared properties you could state; Chinese mathematical classics organized problem types into teachable families; later set language and scientific taxonomies sharpened what “belongs with” means in writing. Once kinds can be written down, copied, and argued over, classification stops being only a pile on a mat and becomes a public agreement about sameness. That agreement is what lets strangers share a ledger without sharing a room. Categories on paper travel farther than categories in a single storeroom—and they invite correction when someone disagrees.
